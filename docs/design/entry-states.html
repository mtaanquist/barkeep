Barkeep · the way in, and the ways it breaks

A sign above a door, not a hero.

The gradient goes. Barkeep's landing is the one screen with room for a statement, so the statement should be the thing the product actually is — a bar sign: an ink field, the word set enormous, a hairline rule, and nothing else competing. It costs nothing, it renders identically in both colour schemes, and it tells a guest they're somewhere with a menu rather than somewhere with a signup flow. Everything downstream inherits that restraint: the errors are quiet, the empty state is honest, and the QR arrival does exactly one thing.

Landing · 1280 and 390

Two panels at 1280, split 44/56: the sign on the left, the work on the right. The sign panel is ink-filled in both schemes — light mode does not lighten it — which is the single gesture that makes the landing feel authored rather than generated, and it costs one colour. The right panel leads with the bar list, because returning to a bar you've used is the common case and creating one is the rare one; Opret en ny bar is a full-width outline button under a rule, not a link, and not a peer of the list. Language sits top-right of the right panel as a plain two-item segmented control — at this point in the product there are no other settings to bury it under, and a guest who needs Danish needs it now, not after signing in. At 390 the sign becomes a 200px band at the top: same type, same rule, cropped rather than redesigned.

Login · one screen, two doors — and Create bar

Not a tab pair. Ninety-odd percent of the people on this screen are guests, and a 50/50 tab makes them read and choose before they can type. So the guest path is the screen — one field, one button, autofocused — and the bartender path is a single muted line under the rule that swaps the panel in place. The bar's name is the title, large, so a guest who scanned the wrong code notices here rather than after ordering. Create bar is the same panel geometry with four fields; the two password fields sit under their own mono kicker with a plain-language line about what each one unlocks, because “bartender password” and “guest password” are meaningless side by side without it. The guest password is optional and says so — most house parties don't want one.

The QR arrival · the most important twelve seconds in the product

Someone standing in a dim kitchen has just pointed a camera at a card and tapped a notification. They have no idea what this is. So the arrival screen answers three questions in the order they're asked — where am I (the bar's name, biggest thing on screen, on the ink field so it reads as the same sign from the landing), what is this (one sentence), what do I do (one field, already focused, keyboard already up). Nothing else: no tour, no permissions, no cookie notice, no "welcome to Barkeep". The name field is pre-filled from localStorage if they've been to any bar before, in which case the button is the only thing they touch. And the menu is already loading behind it — the sheet is 78% height, the top of the drinks list visible above it, so the promise is visible before the form is done.

WHY THE SHEET IS INK, NOT SURFACE

Every other sheet in the guest app (recipe, filters) is a normal surface. This one is the same ink field as the landing sign, and it is the only guest surface that ever is. That does two things: it makes arrival feel like a threshold rather than a form, and it means a returning guest can tell at a glance — before reading anything — whether they're being asked to identify themselves or just look at a recipe.

It also solves dark mode for free. The sheet is ink in both schemes, so the arrival looks identical at 3pm setup and 1am in a dark room, and there is no scheme in which white text on white photo can happen.

WHAT WE DELIBERATELY DIDN'T ADD
· No onboarding carousel. The menu is the explanation. · No notification permission prompt. The dock is the notification, and it doesn't need permission. · No “continue as guest / sign up”. There is no account to make. · No bar-picker fallback. If the QR resolved, the bar is known; if it didn't, that's the error state below.

When it goes wrong · three severities, three shapes

Severity is carried by how much of the screen the message takes, not by colour. A full screen means the app cannot continue. A banner under the header means it is degraded but usable — this one is not danger-coloured, because a dropped websocket at a house party resolves itself nine times in ten and painting it red teaches guests to ignore red. An empty state means nothing is wrong at all. Danger colour is reserved for the two places where something is genuinely lost: a rejected order, and a destructive confirm.
